Quick reference

How to run it.

An enquiry plus the practice's own rate data becomes a fee proposal draft: scope in deliverables, exclusions written as carefully as inclusions, numbered assumptions, a build-up from supplied rates. Never invents a rate, a percentage, an hours figure or a market fee.

Run it

Attach the enquiry and the practice's rate card and resource data:

Draft a fee proposal for this enquiry using our rate card.
Build the scope and exclusions schedule — I'll put the numbers in.
What assumptions does this fee depend on? The brief is vague on submissions.
What do we need to decide before this can go out?

Running it without rate data is often the right move: you get the scope, exclusions, assumptions and additional services schedule with numbers left as [TBC] — which is the hard part of the document anyway.

The proposal

PartWhat it holds
UnderstandingThe commission as the enquiry describes it, with anything unclear flagged rather than assumed.
Scope by stageDeliverables, meetings, submissions, iterations — and the information required from others before each stage can start.
ExclusionsExplicit and itemised. A silent exclusion is not an exclusion.
AssumptionsNumbered, phrased so their failure is visible. Referred to by number for years.
Fee build-upResource by stage from supplied rates; [TBC] wherever either rate or resource is missing.
Additional servicesWhat is charged separately and on what basis — so the conversation happens now.
Programme assumptionsThe duration the fee assumes, and what happens if the project extends.
Internal gap noteEvery [TBC], every director decision, every unevidenced claim. Marked internal. Remove before issue.

Scope is written in deliverables, never adjectives

  • Not "full design services for Stage 3" but "Stage 3: GA plans, sections and elevations at 1:100; outline specification; coordination with [named consultants]; [n] design team meetings; one client presentation".
  • Not "liaison with the planning authority" but "[n] pre-application meetings; one submission; response to [n] rounds of officer comments; resubmission excluded".
  • Never "reasonable amendments". That phrase has cost more fee than any other six characters in the profession.

Where the enquiry does not establish a count, the count is [TBC] and goes in the assumptions. It is never defaulted to a plausible number.

What it will not do — by design

  • Supply a rate, percentage, fee level or hours estimate. Not from memory, not as "typical practice", not as a range, not as a starting point to adjust. This is absolute — an invented rate in a commercial document will be signed.
  • Benchmark against market fees, or say whether a fee is competitive.
  • Decide the fee. It does the arithmetic of the practice's own inputs.
  • Advise on appointment terms, liability, insurance, copyright or limitation. Those go to the internal gap note for your own advisers.
  • Write open-ended scope — "as required", "full service", "reasonable amendments".
  • Default a count. [TBC] instead.
  • Claim experience or accreditation the supplied material does not evidence.
  • Issue. Every output is a draft with an internal section in it.

Work the output

  1. Delete the internal gap note before issuing. The skill says so in chat every time; read that line.
  2. Spend your review time on the exclusions, not the number. That is where the fee is actually lost.
  3. Answer every [TBC] count deliberately — each is a question the enquiry failed to settle, and settling it in your favour now is free.
  4. Check the "information required from others" lines per stage. They are the basis of most extension-of-service claims and almost nobody writes them down.
  5. Keep the numbered assumptions in the issued proposal. Unnumbered assumptions cannot be cited later.
  6. Re-run when the brief changes. A fee built on assumption 4 needs reissuing the moment assumption 4 stops being true.

Remember: the proposal protects you through the things it refuses to leave vague. Every [TBC] is a conversation you get to have now, at no cost, instead of in month four at your own expense.

Triggering

When Claude reaches for it.

This is the description Claude itself reads to decide whether the skill applies. It is worth knowing, because it tells you exactly how to phrase the ask — and what the skill will decline.

fee-proposal-builder

Turn an enquiry plus the practice's own rate and resource data into a fee proposal draft: scope defined stage by stage with exclusions written as carefully as inclusions, a resource build-up from supplied rates, assumptions that the fee depends on, a schedule of additional services, and the commercial terms the practice already uses. It never invents a rate, a percentage, a number of hours or a market fee level. Use this WHENEVER someone asks to "draft a fee proposal", "write the fee letter", "build up the fee for this project", "what's our scope for this", "put together the appointment proposal", "price this enquiry", or needs a scope-and-exclusions schedule before quoting. Do NOT use it to decide what the fee should be, to benchmark against market rates, to supply hourly rates or percentage fees from memory, to advise on appointment terms or liability, or to issue anything.
