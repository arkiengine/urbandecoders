Quick reference

How to run it.

Assembles what a thermal modeller needs: rooms, openable areas as drawn, shading that is actually resolved, ventilation routes that survive the fire strategy, and the constraints on opening a window. Never simulates, never states a criterion, never predicts a result.

Run it

Attach plans, elevations, sections and specification:

Prepare the overheating evidence pack for the thermal modeller.
What do we need to decide before the overheating assessment can run?
Which windows actually open, and what's restricting them?
Our ventilation strategy crosses the corridor — does that survive the fire strategy?

Attach the noise and air quality assessments if they exist. Whether windows can be opened is the input that most often invalidates an overheating study — and it is almost never a thermal question.

The pack

PartWhat it holds
BasisDrawings and specs with revisions, the methodology (or [not stated]), building type and tenure, stage.
Room scheduleEvery space: use, area, orientation(s), storey, exposed elevations, single-aspect and top-floor flagged.
Glazing & openingsPer room: glazed area, frame, opening type, openable area as drawn, restrictor assumption, governing safety requirement.
ShadingExternal shading as drawn, whether a section resolves it, overshadowing from own massing and neighbours.
VentilationThe strategy, the routes it depends on, and every route crossing a compartment line, a closed door or another tenancy.
External constraintsNoise, air quality, security, safety, privacy — each with its source and whether it restricts opening.
Fabric & gainsBuild-ups as specified; occupancy and gains listed as assumptions to be agreed, never as values.
Gaps & decisionsWhat must be decided before the model runs, and by whom.

Tags: [as drawn] · [as specified] · [not resolved] · [decision needed] · [to verify] · [not stated].

The five inputs that go wrong — all architectural, none thermal

  1. The openable area is not what the elevation suggests. A 2.4 m window with a 300 mm top-hung vent is a 300 mm vent.
  2. The window cannot be opened at all, or not at night — noise, air quality, security, guarding. Usually an undecided decision at the time of modelling.
  3. Cross-ventilation is assumed across a fire compartment or through doors that will be closed.
  4. Shading is drawn but not resolved — a brise-soleil in elevation with no section.
  5. Internal gains are the modeller's default, not the client's operation.

What it will not do — by design

  • Predict, estimate or calculate risk. No hours, no degrees, no "likely to pass", no "this orientation will be fine".
  • State a criterion, threshold, weather dataset or temperature limit from memory. The methodology gets named and marked [to verify].
  • State a restrictor dimension, guarding height or free-area percentage from memory.
  • Assume an openable proportion of a glazed area.
  • Recommend mitigation — shading, glazing, ventilation, cooling. Mitigation is a design response that belongs after the assessment.
  • Supply occupancy or internal gains values. The modeller proposes, the client agrees, the pack records who decided.
  • Treat a drawn shading element as resolved without a section.
  • Reassure. "Dual aspect, so it should be fine" is a prediction in prose.

Work the output

  1. Take the [decision needed] opening restrictions to the client and the fire and acoustic consultants first. They collapse more ventilation strategies than anything else.
  2. Section every [not resolved] shading element before the model runs, or the detail will later contradict the result.
  3. Check the ventilation routes against the fire strategy now. A night-purge route through a protected escape corridor will not survive.
  4. Agree occupancy and gains in writing with the client, and keep the record. They are the inputs most often disputed after a failing result.
  5. Send the pack with the fee enquiry. It is the difference between an accurate quote and a provisional one.
  6. Re-run the pack after any elevation change. Openable area is a function of the window schedule, and window schedules move quietly.

Remember: overheating studies get run twice because the architectural inputs arrived late. This pack is the first run's insurance.

Triggering

When Claude reaches for it.

This is the description Claude itself reads to decide whether the skill applies. It is worth knowing, because it tells you exactly how to phrase the ask — and what the skill will decline.

overheating-evidence-pack

Assemble the input pack a thermal modeller needs to run an overheating assessment: the room-by-room schedule of spaces to be assessed, the glazing, shading, opening and ventilation geometry as drawn, the fabric build-ups as specified, the occupancy and internal gains assumptions that must be agreed, the external constraints (noise, pollution, security, safety) that may restrict window opening, and a gaps list — each item naming the document or decision that would settle it. It never runs a simulation, states a criterion or reports a result. Use this WHENEVER someone asks to "prepare for an overheating assessment", "what does the thermal modeller need", "scope the overheating study", "will these flats overheat", "TM59 pack", "Part O evidence", or supplies apartment plans ahead of a thermal consultant's appointment. Do NOT use it to predict, estimate or calculate overheating risk, to state assessment criteria or thresholds from memory, to recommend a mitigation strategy, or to replace a thermal modeller.
