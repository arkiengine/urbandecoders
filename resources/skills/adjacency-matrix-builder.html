Quick reference

How to run it.

A brief becomes a relationship matrix: every rated pair traced to the statement that produced it, contradictions logged, unstated pairs left visibly blank. Records relationships; never arranges them, never fills a blank.

Run it

Attach the brief or schedule of accommodation:

Build an adjacency matrix from this brief at department level.
What relationships has this brief actually stated, and what has it not?
Matrix for the user-group workshop on Thursday — group the questions by department.
Add these workshop answers to the matrix and show me what's still blank.   (iteration)

Say the building type, the stage, and who will answer the queries. The query sheet is ordered for that person, which is what makes it get used.

The output

PartWhat it holds
Relationship matrixTriangular, spaces both axes. A rating where there is a basis; a visibly empty cell where there is none.
Basis registerEach rated pair: the two spaces, the rating, the verbatim statement that produced it, its location.
Conflicts logPairs where two sources imply different ratings. Both quoted, neither resolved.
Query sheetUnrated pairs that matter, grouped so a workshop can clear them in one pass.
Flow notesMovement the matrix cannot express — one-way routes, clean to dirty, public/staff/service.
Diagram briefA written spec for the bubble diagram, for the architect to draw. It does not draw it.

The scale — deliberately coarse, because a finer one invites invented precision:

RatingMeaning
EEssential — direct connection required
IImportant — close, same level or short route
NNeutral — a source established no relationship is needed
SSeparate — distance, barrier or route separation required
XIsolate — must not be adjacent or connected

Markers: * inferred rather than stated, with the inference written out · ? provisional, on the query sheet.

N and blank are not the same. N means someone said no relationship is needed. Blank means nobody has said. Collapsing the two is what makes a matrix dangerous.

What it will not do — by design

  • Complete the matrix. Blanks are the output. On a thin brief the matrix is mostly empty and the query sheet is long — that is the correct answer and the most useful thing to take to a workshop.
  • Rate from building-type convention. That a nurses' station usually overlooks the bay is knowledge about buildings in general, not about this brief. Ask explicitly for conventional adjacencies and they arrive as a separate labelled list that never enters the matrix.
  • Produce a layout, test-fit, stacking diagram or zoning plan. The matrix feeds those; it is not them.
  • Draw the bubble diagram.
  • Resolve a contradiction by choosing the more sensible statement.
  • Invent a space the brief does not name — however obviously the building needs it. A brief with no accessible WC named is a finding, made loudly.
  • Quote a separation distance or standard from memory.

Work the output

  1. Check the granularity before anything else. A 60-space matrix has 1,770 cells and nobody will fill it. Department level first, rooms only where it matters.
  2. Take the conflicts log to the client before the query sheet — contradictions in a brief are usually real disagreements between stakeholders that nobody has surfaced.
  3. Run the query sheet as the workshop agenda. It is grouped by who would know.
  4. Read the flow notes alongside the matrix. Sequence and direction are where the expensive mistakes live, and a matrix cannot express either.
  5. Re-run after the workshop and compare fill rates. A rising rate with a shrinking query sheet is real progress; a full matrix after one meeting means somebody guessed.
  6. Draw the diagram from the brief, not from the matrix alone — and do not let page proximity imply a relationship the matrix declined to assert.

Remember: the matrix that looks finished is the one to distrust. Every blank it preserved is a decision still owned by the people who should be taking it.

Triggering

When Claude reaches for it.

This is the description Claude itself reads to decide whether the skill applies. It is worth knowing, because it tells you exactly how to phrase the ask — and what the skill will decline.

adjacency-matrix-builder

Turn a brief, schedule of accommodation or stakeholder notes into a functional adjacency matrix and relationship diagram brief — every space pair rated for required proximity, separation or isolation, each rating traced to the statement that produced it, with contradictions logged and unstated pairs left visibly blank. Use this WHENEVER someone asks to "build an adjacency matrix", "what needs to be next to what", "bubble diagram", "relationship matrix", "functional adjacencies for this brief", "stack these departments", "which spaces must be separated", or is starting a Stage 1–2 programme study on a complex building type. Do NOT use it to produce a layout, test-fit, plan or stacking solution (it records relationships, never arranges them), to supply adjacency conventions for a building type from memory, or to replace user consultation — an unstated adjacency is a question for the client, not a gap for the skill to fill.
