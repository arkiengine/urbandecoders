Quick reference

How to run it.

Recomputes every total, finds mixed measurement bases and unit drift, reconciles against brief and drawings. Audits a supplied schedule — never re-measures from drawings, never silently corrects a printed figure.

Run it

Attach the schedule, plus the brief and drawings if you have them:

Audit this area schedule before it goes to the client.
Do these numbers add up? The QS says the GIA doesn't match our planning submission.
Check this mix against the brief — I think we've drifted on the two-beds.
This schedule came from the architect we're taking over from. What can we trust?

Say what it is about to be used for — planning, valuation, tender, sales. The verdict's "safe to use for" line is written against that.

The report

SectionWhat it holds
VerdictIssuable as it stands? Safe uses, unsafe uses, the single biggest problem. First, always.
Basis of measurementWhich standard each section uses, stated and inferred, and every place two bases are summed.
Arithmetic checkEvery total, subtotal, percentage and ratio recomputed — as printed / as recomputed / difference.
Unit integrityUnits per column, conversions found, double-conversion suspicions.
Brief reconciliationStated requirement against scheduled provision, both figures visible.
Drawing reconciliationWhat the drawings corroborate and contradict. Counts, never measurements.
Ranked discrepanciesHigh / Medium / Low / Info, each stating what it breaks downstream.
What was not checkedAlways present. The verdict is only as good as this list.

Evidence tags:

TagMeaning
[as printed]Exactly as it appears in the supplied schedule.
[recomputed]Arithmetic on the supplied figures; inputs named.
[inferred]Basis or unit deduced from context, with the deduction stated.
[corroborated]A supplied drawing or document independently supports the row.
[verify manually]Not knowable from what was supplied; names what would settle it.

The most common defect in any area schedule is a row inserted outside the SUM range. It is invisible on a printout and it survives every review that reads rather than recomputes.

The five failures it hunts

  1. Totals that no longer sum — a row outside the range.
  2. Mixed bases — GIA and NIA added into one "total area" that means nothing.
  3. Unit drift — a m² schedule with four ft² rows, or a conversion applied twice.
  4. Mix drift from the brief — 35% two-bed asked, 28% delivered, nobody recomputed.
  5. Arithmetically impossible efficiency for the stated gross.

What it will not do — by design

  • Correct the schedule. Findings are reported; the author edits. An annotated copy marks discrepancies in a new column and leaves originals alone.
  • Measure from drawings. Counting units, levels and uses is allowed. Scaling off to "check" an area is the false confidence this skill prevents.
  • Convert units or reconcile two bases into one.
  • State what a measurement standard includes from memory. It names the code of measuring practice and marks it [verify manually].
  • Quote a space standard or minimum area from memory.
  • Judge whether an area, mix or efficiency is good, marketable or viable. Arithmetic only.
  • Choose between two conflicting figures. Both get reported, with locations.
  • Present a recomputation as the right answer. It is the arithmetic of the supplied numbers; if those are wrong, so is it.

Work the output

  1. Read the verdict. If a High says the schedule is not issuable, stop it going out before you read anything else.
  2. Fix the arithmetic in the source file, not in the audit — then re-run.
  3. Resolve mixed bases with whoever measured. A summed GIA+NIA figure has to be rebuilt, not adjusted.
  4. Take [verify manually] measurement-standard rows to your own copy of the code of measuring practice. The skill deliberately will not recall it.
  5. Send drawing/schedule contradictions back to the drawing author — a scheduled unit with no drawn space is somebody's missing revision.
  6. Re-run before every issue. A schedule that passed at rev C is not a schedule that passes at rev F.

Remember: the audit checks whether the schedule agrees with itself and with what you supplied. Whether it agrees with the building is a measured survey, and that is a different job.

Triggering

When Claude reaches for it.

This is the description Claude itself reads to decide whether the skill applies. It is worth knowing, because it tells you exactly how to phrase the ask — and what the skill will decline.

area-schedule-audit

Audit a supplied area schedule — residential, commercial or mixed-use — for arithmetic, measurement-basis consistency, unit integrity and brief compliance: recomputes every total and subtotal, finds where GIA, NIA, GEA, NSA and IPMS are mixed, reconciles the schedule against drawings and the brief, and returns a verdict-first report with every discrepancy located. It never re-measures from drawings and never silently corrects a printed figure. Use this WHENEVER someone supplies an area schedule, accommodation schedule or unit mix and asks to "check these areas", "audit the area schedule", "do these numbers add up", "why doesn't the GIA match", "check the mix against the brief", "sense-check the areas before it goes out", "is this efficiency plausible", or before any schedule is issued to a client, agent, QS or planning authority. Do NOT use it to measure areas from drawings (it audits a supplied schedule), to transcribe a schedule from an image (screenshot-to-editable-table), to produce a brief's requirements (client-brief-intake), or to give valuation or viability advice.
