Quick reference

How to run it.

Scheme and context drawings become a screening worksheet: which receptors need testing, which methodology applies, what geometry is required, what is missing. Scopes the assessment. Never calculates it, never reports a pass or fail.

Run it

Attach the proposal drawings, any context survey or model, and the site plan:

Screen this scheme for daylight and sunlight — what needs testing?
Which neighbours do we need to assess, and what's missing before a consultant can price it?
We have a massing model and an OS context extrusion. Is that enough to test against?
Scope the internal daylight testing for these apartment plans.

Say which neighbours have objected, and attach the authority's validation checklist or daylight SPD. Local requirements routinely differ from national guidance and the skill will not assume which applies.

The worksheet

PartWhat it holds
Basis of screeningEvery drawing and model supplied, its revision, what it shows, and the coordinate and level datum it uses.
External receptorsNeighbouring windows and amenity areas, by building and elevation, with apparent room use and the test that would apply.
Internal receptorsRooms in the proposal needing testing, with use, level, orientation and glazed area where dimensioned.
Methodology mapWhich named BRE or BS procedure applies to each group — document and clause, marked [to verify].
Geometry requirementsWhat must be measured or modelled before testing, and to what accuracy.
Baseline questionsAs-built / as-consented / cumulative — the questions that change the answer.
Gaps & blockersWhat cannot be screened at all from the supplied material.

Receptor tags:

TagMeaning
[drawn]On a supplied drawing, with drawing and grid reference.
[surveyed]Position and sill/head levels from a measured survey.
[photographic]Visible in a photo only. Position approximate, use unconfirmed.
[assumed use]A window exists; its room use is not evidenced. Use governs whether it is tested.
[not visible]An elevation nothing supplied shows. Needs site or survey.
[to verify]Needs the consultant, the authority or a records search.

Work elevation by elevation around the proposal, never building by building. Buildings get skipped; elevations do not. The [not visible] row is the one that becomes an objection.

Rights to light is a different thing

Planning daylight and sunlight is judged against guidance. Rights to light is a property right, assessed by different specialists, with legal consequences planning consent does not touch. Where screening surfaces a possible right, the worksheet names it as a matter for a rights-to-light surveyor and the client's solicitor — and stops. No assessment, no risk rating, no reassurance.

What it will not do — by design

  • Calculate anything. No VSC, APSH, NSL, ADF, sDA or DF; no percentages, no "approximately". A number in this worksheet is a defect.
  • State a threshold or target from memory. The document and clause get named and marked [to verify]; the consultant supplies the figure.
  • Report a pass, fail, likelihood or risk level.
  • Reassure. "The separation looks generous" is a numerical judgement in prose and must not appear.
  • Assume a room use in order to drop a receptor from the schedule.
  • Treat an extruded OS footprint as a height. That is an assumption and is tagged as one — it is also the most common reason later numbers mean nothing.
  • Replace the consultant. The worksheet exists to brief them properly and make their fee accurate.

Work the output

  1. Resolve the datum and units mismatches first. Nothing downstream is worth running until every source agrees on levels.
  2. Answer the baseline questions with the client and consultant before any test is commissioned — they move results more than most design moves do.
  3. Chase every [not visible] elevation. That is the survey instruction.
  4. Send the geometry requirements list to the consultant with the fee enquiry; it is what makes a daylight fee quote accurate instead of provisional.
  5. Confirm [assumed use] rooms with the neighbours' records or a site visit before anyone relies on a receptor being excluded.
  6. Re-screen after any massing change. The receptor list is a function of the massing, not of the site.

Remember: the dispute is almost never about the method. It is about the window that was never on the list. A long receptor schedule with honest tags is the cheapest insurance in the project.

Triggering

When Claude reaches for it.

This is the description Claude itself reads to decide whether the skill applies. It is worth knowing, because it tells you exactly how to phrase the ask — and what the skill will decline.

daylight-sunlight-screener

Turn supplied scheme and context drawings into a daylight and sunlight screening worksheet: which neighbouring windows and amenity areas need testing, which internal rooms need testing, which BRE/BS methodology applies to each, what geometry must be measured for it, and what is missing before any test can run. It scopes and prepares the assessment; it never calculates VSC, APSH, NSL, ADF or spatial daylight autonomy, and never reports a pass or fail. Use this WHENEVER someone asks to "screen this for daylight and sunlight", "do we need a daylight assessment", "which neighbours are affected", "what do we need for the BRE check", "rights to light risk on this scheme", "scope a daylight study", or supplies massing against neighbouring buildings and asks about overshadowing. Do NOT use it to produce numerical daylight or sunlight results (that needs specialist software and a qualified consultant), to give rights-to-light legal advice, to assess overheating (overheating-evidence-pack), or to judge planning compliance (planning-policy-extract).
